The Case for Drinking More Water

LC
Lisa Chen  ·  PharmD, Clinical Pharmacist
| 3 min read | February 8, 2026

Water is involved in nearly every biological process your body runs: transporting nutrients, regulating temperature, lubricating joints, supporting kidney function, and maintaining the blood volume your heart needs to circulate effectively. It accounts for roughly 60 percent of adult body weight. And yet surveys consistently show that a significant portion of adults are chronically mildly dehydrated without knowing it — a state that impairs concentration, mood, and physical performance before it ever produces the sensation of thirst.

Thirst is a lagging indicator. By the time you feel thirsty, mild dehydration has often already set in. The standard recommendation of eight 8-ounce glasses (about two liters) per day is a reasonable benchmark for most adults in temperate conditions, but your actual needs depend on your body size, activity level, climate, and diet. High-fiber diets, physical activity, heat, and certain medications all increase your water needs. The simplest real-world check: look at the color of your urine. Pale yellow indicates good hydration; dark yellow or amber suggests you need more fluid soon.

The health benefits of adequate hydration extend well beyond not feeling thirsty. Proper hydration supports kidney function, helping flush waste products and reducing the risk of kidney stones — which form more readily when urine is concentrated. It helps regulate blood pressure by maintaining appropriate blood volume and supports digestive function. In the brain, even mild dehydration — a 1 to 2 percent loss of body water — has been shown to impair short-term memory, attention, and psychomotor speed. People who drink more water also tend to consume fewer total calories, partly because thirst is sometimes mistaken for hunger.

Increasing your water intake doesn't require willpower as much as it requires systems. Keep a water bottle on your desk and treat refilling it as a cue. Drink a glass of water first thing in the morning, before meals, and before and after exercise. Eat water-rich foods — cucumbers, lettuce, watermelon, strawberries, and citrus all contribute meaningfully to daily hydration. If plain water feels boring, sparkling water, herbal teas, and water infused with fruit or cucumber all count. Caffeinated beverages like coffee and tea also contribute to hydration despite their mild diuretic effect — the net effect on hydration is positive.

Key Takeaway: You don't need to dramatically increase your water intake overnight. Add one extra glass in the morning and one before each meal. That alone adds meaningful fluid to your daily total and keeps you comfortably hydrated across most conditions.

The goal isn't to count glasses obsessively — it's to make hydration a background habit rather than an afterthought. For most people, a few intentional cues and a reliable water bottle are all it takes to maintain the hydration level their body needs to function well every single day.
