Sleep Health Guide

Sleep Better, Live Better: A Science-Based Guide

SC
Dr. Sarah Chen  ·  MD, Cardiologist
| 5 min read | March 5, 2026

Sleep is not a passive state. While you rest, your brain is cycling through complex restorative processes — consolidating memory, clearing metabolic waste, regulating hormones, and repairing cellular damage. The cardiovascular system lowers its blood pressure during deep sleep; the immune system releases protective cytokines; growth hormone, essential for tissue repair, peaks during slow-wave sleep. When you cut sleep short night after night, all of these processes are disrupted — not just tonight, but cumulatively, across your biology.

Adults generally need seven to nine hours per night, though individual variation exists. The problem is that in a culture that treats sleep as a luxury rather than a biological necessity, many people chronically underslumber without recognizing the toll it takes. Short-term sleep loss manifests as irritability, reduced concentration, and impaired reaction time — the equivalent of legal intoxication at 17 hours of wakefulness. Long-term, chronic sleep deprivation is associated with increased risk of hypertension, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease.

Sleep hygiene — the set of behaviors and environmental conditions that support quality sleep — is the first line of intervention for most sleep problems. Keep a consistent sleep and wake time, including weekends. This regularity anchors your circadian rhythm, the internal 24-hour clock that governs nearly every biological process in your body. Exposure to natural light in the morning helps set this clock; avoiding bright artificial light (especially blue-spectrum screens) in the hour before bed helps wind it down. Keep your bedroom cool — 65 to 68 degrees Fahrenheit is the range most associated with optimal sleep — and as dark as possible.

Caffeine has a half-life of approximately five to seven hours in most adults, meaning a 3 PM coffee is still half-active in your system at 9 PM. Alcohol, while it may help you fall asleep faster, suppresses REM sleep and causes early-morning awakening — net negative for sleep quality even if net positive for initial drowsiness. If you lie awake for more than 20 minutes, get up and do something calm in dim light until you feel sleepy again; this prevents the bed from becoming associated with wakefulness, which is a key driver of insomnia.

Key Takeaway: Sleep quality depends more on consistency than quantity. A regular sleep schedule, a cool dark room, and limiting screens for 60 minutes before bed will improve your sleep more reliably than any supplement on the market.

If sleep problems persist beyond three months, or significantly affect your daytime functioning, they meet the clinical threshold of insomnia disorder and warrant a conversation with your doctor. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the gold-standard treatment — more effective and more durable than sleep medications — and is available from trained therapists and through several validated digital programs covered by many AcmeHealth plans.
