Workspace essentials

Four things, nested inside each other

Project
What you save, name and share. One business question, or a set of related ones.
Panel
A date range and a set of segments, applied to everything inside it. Change the panel date range and every visualization in it moves.
Visualization
A table, a line chart, a fallout. Each one runs its own query.
Component
A dimension, a metric, a segment or a date range. The vocabulary you build with.
Most confusion in Workspace comes from applying something at the wrong level. A segment on a panel changes six charts. The same segment on a table changes one.
Five minutes. Draw the nesting with your hands, it lands better than the slide. The practical payoff is the callout: panel-level versus visualization-level is the single most common source of "why does my number not match yours". Promise them we will come back to it in the segments section this afternoon.

Workspace essentials

Panels do work for you

  • Blank panel. The default. You build everything.
  • Freeform panel. Drop a component and it builds a sensible starting table.
  • Quick insights. Guided prompts for people who do not know where to start. Useful for stakeholders, not for you.
  • Attribution panel. Pick a metric and a dimension and it compares attribution models side by side. We use this after the break.
  • Next or previous item panel. What came immediately before or after a given dimension item.
A panel carries its own date range and its own segments. That is the whole reason to use more than one in a project.
Four minutes. Do not demo all five. Name them, then say that the attribution panel comes back in forty minutes and the next or previous panel comes back tomorrow with flow. The exam does ask you to pick a panel or visualization for a stated business need, so the naming matters.

Workspace essentials

The freeform table, part by part

  • Rows hold dimension items, and optionally segments or date ranges.
  • Columns hold metrics, and optionally segments or date ranges.
  • Every cell is the intersection: this metric, for this row, inside this panel's date range and segments.
  • Right click almost anything. Breakdown, trend, alert, segment from selection, all live in that menu.
Nearly every other visualization in Workspace is a freeform table with a different picture drawn on top of it. Learn this one properly and the rest are quick.

Workspace essentials

Columns carry settings of their own

  • Show a metric as a number, a percentage or both.
  • Turn on a conditional format so the eye finds the outlier before you do.
  • Override the attribution model for that column only, without touching the data view. We use this in the lab after the break.
  • Add the same metric twice with different settings and read the difference across the row.
Column settings are per column, per table. Data view settings are the default for everyone. Knowing which one you changed is the difference between a local experiment and a change your whole team inherits.
Four minutes. The callout is the point and it is a common exam framing: where should this change be made. Local column override for exploration, data view for a definition the organisation should share. Thursday is the day they change the data view.

Workspace essentials

Sorting, filtering, pagination

  • Click a column header to sort. The table re-ranks, it does not re-query the population.
  • Filter the rows by a search term, or by a rule such as contains, starts with, or top ten by a metric.
  • Paginate rather than dragging the row count to 400. High cardinality dimensions are slow and unreadable.
  • A table filter is not a segment. It hides rows. A segment changes who is counted.
That last line gets tested. A filter narrows what you see. A segment narrows who is in the report.
Three minutes. Fast. The filter versus segment distinction is worth stating twice, because people conflate them and then cannot explain why two numbers disagree. It also previews this afternoon.

The point to press: People is a count of stitched persons, so it depends on the stitch working. On an unstitched data view the same report returns a bigger number, because devices have not collapsed into humans. That is Thursday, and it is worth planting now.

Analysis foundations

Scope: three nested containers

Person
Every session and every event for that member, inside the reporting window. Use it when the question is about a human being.
Session
One visit. Use it when the question is about a sitting: did they finish in one go, did they come back.
Event
A single row. Use it when the question is about one specific action.
Worked example. "Members who called the contact centre and completed PCP selection" is a person question, and the two things can be weeks apart. "Sessions where a member called and completed PCP selection" is almost always zero, because a phone call and a web click are not the same session.
Six minutes and it is the most important slide of the morning. The worked example is the one people get wrong in the lab this afternoon and on the exam. Ask the room which container they would use before you reveal it. In Adobe Analytics these were hit, visit and visitor containers; the rename to event, session and person is exam-relevant.

Analysis foundations

A session is a setting, not a fact

  • The data view decides how long an inactivity gap has to be before a new session starts.
  • It can also start a new session when a chosen metric fires, or when a dimension value changes.
  • Change the setting and every session count in every report on that data view changes with it, retroactively.
  • Two data views on the same connection can disagree about how many sessions exist, and both are correct.
This matters for AcmeHealth. Contact centre records arrive as single events. If your session definition is naive, one phone call looks like one session, which inflates session counts for members who call.
Four minutes. Do not go deep, Thursday owns data view configuration. What has to land today is that sessions are constructed at report time from a setting, not stamped on the data at collection. The AcmeHealth callout is a genuine modelling problem and it makes the abstraction concrete.

Analysis foundations

Attribution answers one question

When several dimension items appear before a conversion, which one gets the credit?

  • A member gets the welcome email, ignores it. Gets the 48 hour reminder, opens it. Gets the 8 day reminder, clicks it. Then registers.
  • Three tactics touched one registration. There is one registration and three candidates.
  • Attribution is the rule that distributes the credit. It does not change how many registrations happened.
If only one dimension item appears inside the lookback window, every model returns the same answer. Attribution only does work when there is more than one touch.
Four minutes. The callout is the sentence that stops the most common misunderstanding, which is that attribution is a magic number generator. It only redistributes. Use the room: ask who should get credit for the registration in the example, and let them disagree, because that disagreement is the whole reason models exist.

Analysis foundations

The models you will actually be asked about

ModelWho gets the creditWhen you would use it
Last touch100 percent to the most recent touch before the conversionThe default. Good for closing tactics and short cycles
First touch100 percent to the first touch inside the lookback windowAcquisition and awareness. Which nudge started this
LinearEqual credit to every touchLong cycles, or repeated nudging where every touch plausibly helped
Participation100 percent to every unique touch, so the column can exceed the totalReach questions: which tactics were involved at all
U shaped, J shaped, inverse JWeighted toward first and last, or toward one endWhen you believe the ends matter more than the middle
Time decayMore credit the closer a touch is to the conversionWhen recency genuinely predicts influence
AlgorithmicStatistically derived from the observed dataWhen you have volume and no strong prior
Highlighted rows are the two you will use most and the two the exam leans on. Participation is the trap: it is the only one where the numbers deliberately do not sum to the total.
Six minutes. Do not read the table. Teach last touch and first touch properly, name the rest, and flag participation as the exception that breaks the summing habit people just learned from breakdowns. If someone asks when to use algorithmic, the honest answer is that it needs volume and it is hard to explain to a stakeholder, so use it to check your assumptions rather than to report.

Analysis foundations

The lookback window decides what counts as a touch

  • Session. Only touches in the same session as the conversion.
  • Person. Every touch for that person inside the reporting window.
  • Custom. A fixed number of days back from the conversion.
Where the None line comes from. Conversions with no qualifying touch inside the window are attributed to None. A large None line usually means your window is too short for the real decision cycle, not that the tactic failed.

AcmeHealth enrollment to registration takes a median of eleven days. A session lookback on that funnel would put most of the credit nowhere.

Five minutes. The None line is the single most useful diagnostic on this slide and it appears in the lab, so make sure they know what it means before they see it. The eleven day figure is from the baseline data and it makes the point that the window has to match the behaviour, not the reporting habit.

Analysis foundations

Persistence: attribution's twin, for dimensions

ALLOCATION

Which value to keep when more than one could persist at the same time.

  • Most recent. The value persists until a later value overwrites it.
  • Original. The first value wins and later ones are ignored.

EXPIRATION

How long a value keeps attaching itself to later events.

  • Session or person.
  • A custom time period.
  • When a chosen metric fires, which expires it immediately.
Metrics get attribution. Dimensions get persistence. They do the same job from opposite ends, and they are configured in the same place, the data view.
Six minutes. The pairing in the callout is the mental model worth carrying into Thursday and into the exam. Persistence is off by default, which surprises people: without it, a dimension only relates to metrics on the same event. Give the AcmeHealth example out loud: if tactic name does not persist, a registration that happened two days after the email click is credited to nothing at all.

Analysis foundations

Binding, when persistence is too blunt

  • A binding dimension ties a persisted value to the value of another dimension, so it only applies while that other value holds.
  • A binding metric ties it to a metric firing, so the value attaches to that event and stops.
  • Without binding, most recent allocation across a person can smear one value across activity it had nothing to do with.
Name it today, use it Thursday. What you need now is to recognise the symptom: a dimension value showing up against conversions it could not plausibly have caused.
Four minutes, and resist going deeper. Binding is genuinely hard and it belongs in the Thursday data view session. The goal here is recognition, not fluency. If the room is strong, use the Adobe worked example of a search term persisting across unrelated profiles on a shared account.

  • One schema field can become several components. Registration completes with a first touch model and registration completes with a last touch model are two components from one field.
  • A component that does not exist in the data view does not exist for the analyst. That is not a bug, it is curation.

Five minutes. This is the slide that connects yesterday's schema lesson to today's rail, so make the callback explicit: the field paths they saw on the Day 1 schema slide are these components. The one-field-to-many-components point is the setup for both calculated metrics later today and the data view work on Thursday.

Components

Dimensions: no eVars, no props

  • CJA uses XDM, so there is no fixed inventory of variables to ration. Any string field can be a dimension.
  • That freedom has a cost: cardinality. A dimension with 400,000 unique values is technically valid and practically useless.
  • Good dimensions are low cardinality and readable. Page name, journey step, plan name, region, call queue, tactic name.
  • Bad dimensions are raw URLs, full query strings and IDs. Classify them into something readable, which is Thursday's work.
  • eVars and props→Any XDM field, unlimited
  • Classifications→Lookup datasets
  • Customer attributes→Profile datasets
  • Virtual report suites→Data views
Five minutes. The rename table earns its place for anyone with Adobe Analytics history, and those renames are fair exam material. The cardinality warning is not theoretical: the AcmeHealth call handle time field is a raw number of seconds and it produces hundreds of rows, which they will see on Thursday and fix with bucketing.

Components

Two kinds of component in the rail

STANDARD
Provided by CJA
People, sessions, events, session number, event depth, time between events, dataset ID. Derived by CJA from the event stream, not carried in your schema.
SCHEMA
Built from your fields
Page name, journey step, plan name, call queue, tactic name. Everything specific to AcmeHealth.
Standard components are tied to event datasets. Knowing which of the two you are holding explains most "why is this empty" moments, especially on rows sourced from profile or lookup datasets.
Four minutes. The empty-cell explanation is the practical value here and it comes up in the components hunt lab in twenty minutes. If a standard metric is blank against a lookup-sourced row, that is expected behaviour rather than broken data.

Components

Metrics, and the numbers that are not metrics

  • A metric counts or sums. Events, people, sessions, call handle time, a count of anything.
  • A numeric field is not automatically a metric. Call handle time is a number, but "how many calls took 240 seconds" is a dimension question.
  • In the data view you choose. The same field can be a metric in one component and a dimension in another.
  • As a dimension, a numeric field usually wants value bucketing, or you get one row per distinct number.
The trick worth knowing: a dimension can be used as a metric to count its distinct values. Person ID as a metric answers "how many members do we have", which is a question a lot of people ask and few can answer quickly.
Five minutes. Both directions of this slide are exam material: numerics as dimensions, and dimensions counted as metrics. The callout gets a visible reaction from most rooms, so leave a beat after it. Bucketing is named here and built on Thursday.

Components

Names are for humans, IDs are for portability

  • Every component has a friendly name, inherited from the schema and overridable per data view.
  • Every component also has an ID, which is what the reporting API and Report Builder use.
  • Set the same ID on equivalent components in different data views and a project moves between them without breaking.
  • Without that, a project built on one data view can open on another and show nothing at all.
Descriptions matter more than they look. Twenty five analysts sharing a rail will invent twenty five meanings for an unlabelled metric.
Four minutes. Component IDs are dry but they are on the exam and they explain a real failure mode people will hit when they share projects. The description point is a governance habit worth pushing, and it links forward to project curation on Wednesday.

Section 4

Calculated metrics

Building the number that question 12 needed and nobody had.

Divider. Land it on the callback: they just failed to answer question 12 cleanly, and this section is why.

Calculated metrics

A metric you define, not one you were given

  • Built from other metrics, operators, functions and segments. Saved as a component like any other.
  • Calculated at report time, so it applies to history as soon as you save it. Nothing is reprocessed.
  • Lives in the component rail, can be shared, tagged, approved and curated.
  • It is not a data view change. You are not altering what the field means for everyone.
Rate, ratio, per-person average, difference between two segments. If a stakeholder keeps asking for a number that takes you three clicks to assemble, it should be a calculated metric.
Four minutes. The report-time point is worth pressing because it is the opposite of how most legacy tooling behaves, and it is genuinely liberating: define it today, and last quarter has it too. Draw the line to Thursday: calculated metrics are non-admin and live in the rail, derived fields are admin and live in the data view.

Calculated metrics

What you can put in the builder

  • Metrics, combined with plus, minus, times and divide.
  • Static numbers, for targets and conversions.
  • Functions, including mean, median, count distinct, and a long statistical list.
  • Segments, dropped directly onto a metric so it only counts that population.
  • Other calculated metrics, which is how you keep definitions in one place.
Example
GMW Step Completes ÷ GMW Step Starts

Format as a percentage, set one decimal place, name it GMW Completion Rate, write a description that says what it excludes.

Registration Completes [Opened a nudge]

A segment applied inside the metric. The table can stay unsegmented while one column is narrowed.

The segment-inside-a-metric pattern is the one that converts people. It lets a single table hold "everyone" and "this group" side by side without splitting the report.
Six minutes. Demo the segment drop onto a metric if you have the sandbox open, because it is hard to picture from a slide and it is the technique they will reuse constantly. Mention that descriptions are not optional: a rate metric with no stated denominator will be misread within a week.

Calculated metrics

Formatting is part of the definition

  • Format: decimal, percent, currency, time. A rate shown as 0.62 will be read as sixty two of something.
  • Decimal places: one is usually right. Three implies a precision the data does not have.
  • Show upward trend as: green or red. A rising abandonment rate should not be green.
  • Attribution can be set inside a calculated metric, so the same formula can exist in a first touch and a last touch version.
These are not cosmetics. A badly formatted metric gets misread, and the misreading travels further than you do.
Five minutes. The upward trend colour setting sounds trivial and is the one that gets a laugh and then gets used. Tie attribution back to the morning: the same formula with two models is two components, which is the one-field-many-components idea again.

Segments

Dividing the population, and getting the container right.

Divider.

Segments

Called filters once, called segments now

  • CJA originally renamed segments to filters, to avoid colliding with Experience Platform segments.
  • That has been reversed. The interface says segments again, and the documentation has caught up.
  • Older tutorials, older screenshots and some exam-era material still say filters. They mean this.
  • A table filter is still a different thing. It hides rows. A segment changes who is counted.
Worth ninety seconds because the room will meet both words this week, and the exam guide itself still uses the older one.
Four minutes. Say it once, clearly, then use segments consistently for the rest of the week. If anyone has done the official prep guide they will have seen filter and should not be left wondering whether it is a different feature.

Segments

Same three containers, now inside a definition

Person
Include the whole person if the rule is ever true.
Session
Include sessions where the rule is true.
Event
Include only the events where the rule is true.

One rule, three containers, three very different populations.

Container"Started PCP selection"People
PersonEveryone who ever started it2,200
SessionSessions containing a start2,200

People is identical here, because the rule is a single event. The difference appears the moment you add a second condition.

The one that catches everyone. "Started PCP selection and never completed it" in an event container returns nothing, because no single event can both be a start and not be a complete. It needs a person container.
Six minutes and the callout is the highest value sentence of the afternoon. Say it, then say it again differently. The table is deliberately boring until the second condition arrives, which is the pedagogical point: containers look interchangeable until they very much are not. This exact mistake is waiting in lab 2.5 and on the exam.

Segments

Sequential segments: order, and gaps

  • Then puts the conditions in order. A happened, later B happened.
  • Within and after put a time boundary on the gap: within 7 days, after 1 hour.
  • Only before and only after restrict what may sit between the steps.
  • Exclude at the end of a sequence is how you express "and then did not".
Definition · abandoned PCP selection
Person container
Step 1 · GMW Step = Select PCP, Step Status = start
THEN, within 7 days
Exclude · GMW Step = Select PCP, Step Status = complete

836 people on the baseline data. That is the number from the completion rate table, arrived at from the other direction.

Seven minutes, the longest teaching slide of the day, and it earns it. Build this definition live if you can. The two things people get wrong: putting the exclude inside an event container, and forgetting the time boundary so that someone who completed the step six weeks later is still excluded. Point out that 836 matches the completion rate table exactly, which is a good confidence check and a good habit.

Segments

Where you drop it changes what it does

  • On a panel: every visualization inside it is narrowed.
  • On a table column: that column only, which is how you compare populations side by side.
  • On a row: the segment becomes a row, like a dimension item.
  • Inside a calculated metric: the metric itself only counts that population.
  • On a data view: everyone using it, always. That is an administrative decision, not an analytical one.
Stacked segments are combined with AND. Two segments on one panel means both must be true, which is a common cause of an unexpectedly empty report.
Four minutes. This is the callback to the Workspace anatomy slide from this morning, so make the connection explicit. The stacking rule is the practical fix for the most common panic of the afternoon, which is a table that has gone blank.

Segments

Quick segments, for when you are exploring

  • Built inline in the project, up to three rules, no leaving what you are doing.
  • They live in the project, not the rail, unless you deliberately save them out.
  • Right click a row or a data point and make one from what you selected.
Use them while you are thinking. Promote them to real segments once you know the definition is worth keeping, and then write a description.
Three minutes. The right click route is the one to demo. The habit to encourage is explore with quick segments, commit with saved ones, because a rail full of half-thought-out segments is worse than a rail with none.

Audiences

The first thing this week that leaves CJA.

Divider. Short section, high impact, do not let it get squeezed by an overrunning lab.

Audiences

A segment describes. An audience travels.

SEGMENT
A definition, inside CJA
A rule evaluated at report time. It narrows reports. It never leaves the analytics tool, and nothing downstream can act on it.
AUDIENCE
A membership list, published to Experience Platform
The people who matched, written to the Real-Time Customer Profile so Journey Optimizer, Real-Time CDP and destinations can use them.
  • You build an audience from a segment. Up to twenty segments can feed one audience.
  • Publish it once, or schedule it to refresh. Scheduled refreshes are capped for the whole organisation, so schedule deliberately.
  • Audience publishing sends people out of CJA. Audience analysis is the opposite: bringing Experience Platform audience membership in as a dimension.
This is the sentence to remember for the exam and for your own work: a segment answers a question, an audience makes something happen.
Five minutes. The publishing against analysis distinction is a documented pair and an easy exam question, so state both directions. On the cap: our package allows 100 refreshing audiences for the whole organisation, which is why the lab uses a one time publish. One time publishes do not count against it.

Audiences

What happens when you press publish

  1. CJA evaluates the segment and resolves the people who match.
  2. The membership is written to the Real-Time Customer Profile in Experience Platform.
  3. It appears in the Audiences list there, and becomes available to Journey Optimizer, Real-Time CDP and destinations.
  4. The audience manager shows you how long it took to build, and how long it took to become available downstream.
Publication takes minutes, not seconds. Say that to stakeholders before they ask, because the gap between pressing publish and seeing it in Platform is where confidence gets lost.

The datasets behind an audience do not have to be profile enabled. You can discover an audience in historical event data and still publish it.

Five minutes. The last line surprises people and is worth saying: historical data that was never enabled for profile can still produce a publishable audience, which is one of the genuinely strong arguments for CJA sitting next to Real-Time CDP. Friday picks this audience back up in Journey Optimizer, so tell them their own audience will be targeted on Friday.
