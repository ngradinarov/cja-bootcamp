Managing Stress Before It Manages You

JO
Dr. James Okafor  ·  PsyD, Clinical Psychologist
| 6 min read | February 14, 2026

Stress is not inherently bad. In short bursts, the body's stress response — the surge of cortisol and adrenaline, the heightened alertness, the narrowed focus — is a finely tuned survival system. The problem is that modern workplaces, with their relentless notifications, deadline pressure, and always-on expectations, can put that system into a state of near-constant activation. When stress becomes chronic, the physiological toll is real: elevated blood pressure, disrupted sleep, impaired immune function, and a measurably higher risk of anxiety and depression.

The workplace is one of the most common sources of chronic stress in adults, and the effects don't stay at the office. Research consistently shows that people who report high workplace stress carry that activation home — it impairs sleep quality, strains relationships, and erodes the very recovery time the body needs to reset. The first step in managing this is recognition: understanding that feeling overwhelmed is not a personal failing but a predictable response to an environment that often demands more than a human nervous system was designed to sustain.

There are evidence-based strategies that genuinely help. Structured breaks — even a five-minute walk or deliberate away-from-screen pause every 90 minutes — help the nervous system down-regulate before stress accumulates to a level that's hard to recover from. Setting clear "off" hours for email and digital communication, where possible, has been shown to significantly reduce perceived stress and improve sleep quality. Physical movement remains one of the most potent stress-management tools available, with even 20 minutes of moderate exercise producing measurable reductions in cortisol.

Cognitive reframing — the practice of consciously examining stressful thoughts and testing whether they're accurate — is a technique drawn from cognitive behavioral therapy that you can apply without a therapist. When you catch yourself thinking "I'm going to fail this project," pause and ask: what's the evidence? What would I tell a friend in this situation? This isn't toxic positivity; it's the practice of reducing the emotional amplification that stress tends to trigger, leaving you better able to address the actual problem with clarity rather than panic.

Key Takeaway: Chronic stress is cumulative — it builds on itself if unchecked. Interrupting it with short breaks, movement, and brief periods of genuine disconnection is not laziness; it's maintenance of your most important work asset: your capacity to think clearly.

If stress feels persistent, intense, or is affecting your daily functioning, talking to a mental health professional is a meaningful step, not a last resort. Many AcmeHealth plans include behavioral health benefits, and many providers now offer telehealth options that make scheduling far easier. Your stress response was designed to protect you — with the right tools, you can make sure it stays in that role.
