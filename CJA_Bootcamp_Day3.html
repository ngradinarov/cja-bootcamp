Visualizations

The trend family

  • Line. One or more metrics over time. The only visualization that can show anomalies.
  • Multi line. Separate y axes, for metrics on different scales. Useful, easy to abuse.
  • Area and stacked area. Composition over time. Stacked area hides individual movement, so use it for share rather than for change.
  • Granularity is a property of the panel's date range: day, week, month. Anomaly detection behaves differently at each.
Line charts are where you will spend the AI block this afternoon, because the confidence band only renders on a line.
Five minutes. The line and anomaly link is worth planting here so the afternoon has somewhere to land. Warn against stacked area for trend questions: people reach for it because it is pretty and then cannot answer whether a series moved.

Visualizations

Summary number and summary change

  • Summary number shows one figure, large. It is the most read thing in any project.
  • Summary change shows the same figure against a comparison period, with direction and percentage.
  • Both take their value from a cell in a table, so the table has to be right first.
  • Label them. A large number with no caption will be quoted out of context within a week.
For a stakeholder deck, one summary change and one line chart will outperform a page of tables. Restraint is a reporting skill.
Four minutes. The dependency on an underlying cell trips people up: they delete the table and wonder why the number vanished. Demo that if you have a second. The restraint point matters for this audience, who mostly build for other people.

  • Bullet compares a metric to one or more thresholds you set. Compact, and it reads at a glance.
  • Gauge does the same thing with more ink and less precision.
  • Thresholds are yours to define. CJA does not know what good looks like for AcmeHealth.
  • For today, use 35 percent as the guided member welcome completion target.
Current completion is 43.1 percent overall, so the programme is ahead of target while a single step inside it is failing. Both statements are true, which is exactly why one number is never enough.

Visualizations

Intelligent captions

  • Generative AI writes a plain language summary of what a visualization shows.
  • Supported on line, multi line, bar, horizontal bar, donut, area, flow and fallout.
  • Useful for stakeholders who will not read a chart, and for sanity checking your own reading of one.
  • It describes what is in the chart. It does not know why, and it does not know your business.
Treat a caption as a second opinion, not as an answer. If it disagrees with your reading, one of you has misread the chart and it is worth finding out which.
Four minutes. Turn one on live if the sandbox allows. Keep the framing honest: it is a description generator, and the value is in catching your own misreadings rather than in outsourcing interpretation. This also previews the AI block after the second break.

Section 2

Fallout, flow and cohorts

Three ways of asking what happened next.

Divider.

Fallout

Fallout answers one question

Of the people who reached step one, how many reached step two, and step three, and so on to the end?

  • You define the path. Fallout does not discover it.
  • Each touchpoint can be a dimension item, a metric, or a segment. They can come from different datasets, which is the cross-channel part.
  • The output is a count and a percentage at every step, plus the drop between steps.
  • Right click any step to segment, trend or break down the people who fell out there.
Use fallout when you already believe in the path. Use flow when you want to find out what the path actually is.
Five minutes. The callout is the distinction people get wrong and the exam tests. Press the point that touchpoints can span datasets: a fallout that goes enrollment, web registration, then a phone call is doing something no single-channel tool can do, and it is the argument for CJA in one visualization.

Fallout

Three settings that change the answer

CONTAINER
Person or session
Person lets the path span weeks. Session requires it all in one sitting. For AcmeHealth onboarding, person is almost always right.
PATH TYPE
Eventual or exact
Eventual allows other events in between. Exact requires the next touchpoint to be the very next event. Eventual is the default and usually what you mean.
COMPARISON
A second series
Drop a segment onto the fallout to overlay a second population and read both drop-off curves against each other.
Exact path on a web journey nearly always returns near zero. Real people load a page, go back, check something else, then continue. If your fallout collapses at step two, check this setting before you check the data.
Seven minutes. The exact against eventual trap is the single most useful debugging tip in the whole visualization section, and it is a plausible exam question. The comparison series is what lab 3.2 is built around, so introduce it properly here rather than as an afterthought.

Flow

Flow answers the opposite question

Given that people were here, where did they actually go?

  • You pick a starting point. Flow discovers the paths outward, ranked by volume.
  • Forward, backward, or both. Two steps, three steps, as far as you want.
  • Nodes can be dimension items or metrics, and can come from any dataset in the connection.
  • Click any node to expand it, or to make it the new centre.
Fallout tests a hypothesis. Flow generates one. After the last lab you know 836 people leave at select PCP. Flow tells you where they went.
Five minutes. The pairing with fallout is the point: they have just measured a hole, and now they find out what is on the other side of it. Keep the framing as hypothesis testing against hypothesis generation, because that is the language analysts will reuse.

Flow

The settings that matter

  • Container. Person or session, same meaning as fallout. Session flow stops at the end of the visit.
  • Include repeat instances. On, a page that reloads shows as a loop back to itself. Off, consecutive repeats collapse. Turn it off for readability, on for diagnosing loops.
  • Entry and exit nodes. Flow marks where people arrived from outside and where they left entirely.
  • Dimension choice. Page name gives you navigation. Journey step gives you progress. They tell different stories.
A big exit node next to a failing step is the finding. It means people did not go elsewhere in the product, they left.
Six minutes. Repeat instances is the setting that makes a flow readable or unreadable, so demo the toggle if you can. The exit node point is what makes the next lab land, because the answer includes a substantial exit and that is worse news than a detour.

Cohort tables

The third question: do they come back?

  • Retention. Of the people who did X in a period, how many did Y in each later period.
  • Churn. The inverse view, showing who stopped.
  • Latency. How long between the inclusion event and the return event.
  • Custom. Choose the inclusion metric and the return metric independently.
Cohorts need time. A 90 day window gives you roughly twelve weekly cohorts, which is enough to see a pattern and not enough to be certain of one.
Three minutes, named rather than taught, because guided analysis covers retention after lunch with less setup. The honest caveat about 90 days is worth saying: this is a training sandbox, and in production they would want a year.

Journey canvas

The whole cross-channel journey, in one picture, with branches.

Divider. This is the centrepiece of the day.

Journey canvas

Why not just use fallout?

FALLOUT
One straight line
A single ordered sequence. Every person either continues or drops. No branches, no alternative routes, no rejoining.
JOURNEY CANVAS
A map with junctions
Multiple entry points, branching decision points, paths that split and rejoin. Shows both fallout and fallthrough at every node.
  • Drag and drop nodes onto a canvas and connect them.
  • A node can be an event, a dimension item, a segment or a date range.
  • You can build a journey from scratch, or pull one in from Journey Optimizer and measure the journey somebody actually designed.
  • Exclude nodes, compare the journey to a prior period, and create segments and audiences from any node's fallout.
Six minutes. The Journey Optimizer link is the one to emphasise for this audience: on Friday they build an AJO journey, and the canvas is how they will measure whether it worked. That closes the loop between the two products and it is a genuine differentiator worth knowing about.

Journey canvas

What a node can be

  • An event. Registration complete, a checklist step, a call record.
  • A dimension item. A specific page, a specific tactic, a specific call queue.
  • A segment. Which lets you put "members who completed the survey" in the middle of a path.
  • A date range. For journeys where timing is the condition.
Because a node can be a segment, the canvas can express things a funnel cannot: enrolled, then either opened a nudge or did not, then registered. Two routes to the same outcome, measured separately.
Five minutes. The segment-as-node idea is what unlocks the interesting journeys and it is not obvious, so give it the callout and an example. This is also the setup for the branching structure they build in the lab.

Journey canvas

Fallout and fallthrough at every node

  • Fallthrough is the people who continued from this node to the next one you connected.
  • Fallout is everyone else who reached this node and went no further along that path.
  • Select a node to see the top dimension items that come before or after it, at any point in the journey rather than only immediately.
  • Compare the whole journey to four weeks ago, two quarters ago, a year ago, or a custom range.
  • Turn any node's fallout into a segment, a trend, an audience or a breakdown, without leaving the visualization.
That last point is the workflow: find the leak on the canvas, turn it into an audience, and hand it to Journey Optimizer. You will do exactly that on Friday.
Five minutes. Emphasise the node-to-audience path because it is the through-line of the week and it is what makes this more than a reporting exercise. The comparison feature is newer and worth demoing if the sandbox has data a year back, which ours does not, so say so rather than pretending.

Guided analyses

The query rail

  • Events are what you measure. Labelled steps, key indicators or start and return events depending on the analysis.
  • Segments are who you measure, identified as A, B and so on in the chart.
  • Counted as sets the counting method.
  • Filters narrow an event by a dimension without leaving the rail.
  • Breakdown splits the result, where the analysis supports it.

Time and monitoring

Date ranges that do not need editing, and notes that survive you.

Divider.

Date ranges

Fixed, rolling and relative

FIXED
13 Jun to 11 Sep
Exact dates that never move. Right for a report about a specific period. Wrong for anything scheduled, because it will still be showing June next March.
ROLLING
Last 30 days
Recalculated every time the project opens. Right for monitoring. This is what most scheduled reports should use.
RELATIVE
This month so far
Anchored to a calendar boundary rather than a count of days. Right when the business thinks in months and quarters.
The most common reporting failure in any analytics tool is a scheduled report with a fixed date range. It keeps arriving, it keeps being read, and it stopped being true months ago.
Six minutes. The callout is the highest value warning of the afternoon and it connects directly to the scheduling section later. Ask how many people have received a stale dashboard; most hands go up. Relative against rolling is a genuine exam distinction, so be precise about the calendar boundary.

Date ranges

Save a date range as a component

  • A custom date range lives in the component rail next to metrics and dimensions.
  • Build it once, use it in every project, share it with the team.
  • Drag one into a table column to compare periods inside a single table.
  • Name it for what it means, not for what it is: "Open enrollment window" rather than "Oct 15 to Dec 7".
Naming matters here more than anywhere. A date range called "last quarter" that is actually fixed to Q2 will mislead everyone who uses it.
Five minutes. Date ranges as draggable components surprises people who assume the calendar picker is the only route. The naming warning is real and worth ninety seconds, because these get shared across teams and the definition travels without the context.

Date ranges

Comparison ranges

  • Add a comparison to a panel and every visualization in it gains a second series.
  • Previous period, same period last year, or a custom range.
  • Summary change reads directly from the comparison, which is why it is the fastest headline in the tool.
  • You can show or hide the comparison columns without removing the comparison.
Our sandbox has 90 days of data, so a year-on-year comparison returns nothing. Say that out loud in the room rather than letting somebody conclude the feature is broken.
Four minutes. The honesty callout matters: somebody will try year-on-year and get an empty chart, and if you have not pre-empted it they will spend five minutes debugging a limitation of the training data rather than a bug.

Annotations

Notes that stick to the data

  • Attach a note to a date or a date range. It appears on every chart covering that period, for everyone.
  • Scope it to a metric, a dimension or the whole data view.
  • Colour coded, so a release, an outage and a campaign can be told apart at a glance.
  • This is how institutional memory survives people leaving.
Six months from now, nobody will remember why registrations halved for three days in August. An annotation is the difference between an explained dip and a recurring mystery.
Five minutes. The institutional memory framing lands with this audience, who mostly inherit dashboards from people who left. The August dip is the worked example and they annotate it in the very next activity, so the setup and the payoff are two slides apart.

Section 6

AI capabilities

Anomaly detection, alerts, and what the assistant can and cannot do.

Divider.

Anomaly detection

What it does

  • Builds a statistical expectation for a metric from its own history, then flags the days that fall outside it.
  • Produces an expected value and a confidence band, plotted on a line chart.
  • Anything outside the band is an anomaly, with the size of the departure quantified.
  • It knows nothing about your business. It knows what this metric normally does.
You annotated the August dip because you already knew it was there. Anomaly detection is how you find the next one without knowing to look.
Five minutes. The sequencing across the last two sections is the teaching device, so make it explicit. Keep the definition tight: expectation from history, band around it, flag departures. The business-blindness point matters because it prevents people treating flags as conclusions.

Anomaly detection

How it builds the expectation

  • At daily granularity it uses a reference period of up to 35 days before your reporting window, plus the matching window a year earlier where that exists.
  • It selects between time series models and an outlier detection approach depending on the data.
  • It accounts for weekday and weekend rhythm, and for a set of known holidays.
  • Hourly, weekly and monthly granularities use different techniques.
This is why our sandbox holds 90 days rather than a fortnight. Without 35 days of history behind the reporting window there is nothing to build an expectation from, and every day looks normal.
Six minutes. The 35 day figure is the single most practically important number in the section and it is a plausible exam question. It also explains a real failure mode: a newly connected dataset produces no anomalies for over a month and people assume the feature is broken. The callout ties it to a decision made when the training data was built.

Contribution analysis

The follow-up question

  • Anomaly detection tells you something happened. Contribution analysis looks for which dimension items moved with it.
  • Run it against a daily anomaly and embed the result in the project.
  • It returns correlations, ranked. It does not return causes.
  • A dimension item that barely moved but is enormous can still dominate the result. Read the lift, not just the rank.

Four minutes if entitled, skip if not. The correlation caveat is the whole professional point; contribution analysis output gets quoted as root cause in status meetings constantly. If the entitlement is missing, say so honestly and move on rather than describing a feature they cannot open.

  • Delivered by email or SMS, with a link to a generated Workspace project showing the trigger.
  • Create one directly from a freeform table row.

Alerts

Why there are no hourly alerts in CJA

  • Event data from multiple sources is not complete for a given hour until well after that hour has passed.
  • Adobe's guidance puts typical completeness at three to nine hours past event time.
  • An hourly alert would therefore fire on partial data and report a collapse that is just latency.
  • Adobe Analytics has hourly alerts. CJA does not. The difference is the multi-source architecture.
This is a favourite exam question, and it is also the honest answer when a stakeholder asks for real time alerting on batch data.
Three minutes. Small slide, high exam value, and it connects back to Monday's batch latency lesson. The stakeholder framing is useful: the reason is architectural, not a missing feature, and saying that clearly is more credible than promising to look into it.

AI capabilities

AI assistant, and what to expect from it

  • Natural language questions against your data view. "What are my top pages this month."
  • Good at simple, well specified, single-step questions.
  • Weak where your components are badly named, because it has only your metadata to work from.
  • Everything you were told about descriptions on Tuesday pays off here.
The quality of the assistant is a direct function of the quality of your data view. A rail full of components called "eVar12 copy" produces bad answers, and the fix is curation rather than a better model.
Five minutes. The callout is the argument that turns a governance chore into something people actually do. Demo one question if the sandbox supports it, and pick one that works; a live failure here undersells a genuinely useful feature and eats time.

Section 7

Report distribution

Twelve percent of the exam, and the part that decides whether any of this was worth doing.

Divider. Do not let this section get squeezed; it is a full exam domain and it is the one people skip.

Distribution

Sharing a project

  • Can edit. A full collaborator. They can change your definitions.
  • Can duplicate. They can copy it and work on their own version, leaving yours intact.
  • Can view. Read only, and the safest default for a stakeholder.
  • Share with individuals, with groups, or generate a link.
  • Recipients need access to the data view, not just the project. Sharing a project with someone who cannot see the data view gives them an error, not a report.
That last line is the most common support ticket in any CJA rollout. Check data view access before you blame the share.
Five minutes. The data view access point is the practically useful one and it is exam relevant. Name the three levels precisely, because the exam distinguishes them and people conflate duplicate with edit.

Distribution

Curation: hiding what they do not need

  • Curate a project to a chosen set of components. Recipients see only those in the rail.
  • It makes a project usable for a non-analyst, who would otherwise face four hundred components.
  • Curation is presentation, not security. It hides components from a view; it does not prevent access.
  • If a field must not be seen, that is a data governance and permissions job, which is tomorrow.
The exam tests this distinction. Curate for clarity. Restrict for confidentiality. They are different tools with different owners.
Five minutes. The curation against security distinction is a named exam objective and a genuine risk: people curate a project to hide a sensitive field and believe they have secured it. Tomorrow's governance lab shows what actual restriction looks like, so plant the link now.

Distribution

Scheduling

  • Send a project as a PDF or CSV on a recurring schedule, to people inside or outside the organisation.
  • Daily, weekly, monthly, or a custom cadence, with an end date.
  • Schedules expire. The owner is warned before they do.
  • Use a rolling date range. A scheduled report on a fixed range is a recurring lie.
Before you schedule anything, ask who reads it and what they do differently because of it. Most scheduled reports fail that question.
Four minutes. The rolling date range warning is the callback to the date range section and it is the single most common real world error, so repeat it deliberately. The closing question is worth asking the room; it usually produces a rueful laugh and a real conversation about report hygiene.

Exports

Once data leaves CJA it leaves your governance with it. That is not a reason to avoid exporting, but it is a reason to know which exports exist and who is running them.
Four minutes. The governance framing bridges into tomorrow. For a health plan audience the point lands harder than usual, because an exported CSV of member level data is a genuine risk and somebody in the room will already be worrying about it.
